Before streaming platforms, digital downloads, and even nationwide music charts became part of everyday life, South Korea had its own unique music ecosystem known as **Gilboard (길보드)**.

A combination of the Korean word *gil* (길, meaning "street") and *Billboard*, Gilboard referred not to an official music chart but to the vibrant street culture surrounding cassette vendors who sold unofficial compilation tapes throughout the late 1980s and early 1990s.

More than simply a marketplace, Gilboard became an important gateway through which many Koreans discovered the latest domestic hits alongside Western pop, Eurodance, hip-hop, New Jack Swing, Japanese music, and Hong Kong pop. Although it existed outside the official music industry and relied heavily on informal duplication, Gilboard played a significant role in shaping the musical tastes of an entire generation before the emergence of modern K-pop.

Today, it is remembered as one of the most distinctive chapters in Korea's pre-digital music history.

# Why Was It Called "Gilboard"?

The name Gilboard combines the Korean word *gil* ("street") with *Billboard*, reflecting its role as an unofficial street-based music chart rather than a formally organized ranking.

Unlike Billboard, Gilboard had no central organization or standardized methodology. Instead, cassette vendors displayed handwritten lists of their best-selling tapes, giving customers an idea of which songs were currently popular in local neighborhoods.

Over time, these informal rankings became collectively known as the Gilboard Chart.

---

# The Sound of Korea's Streets

One of the defining images of Gilboard was the mobile cassette cart.

Rather than operating from conventional record stores, many vendors sold tapes from pushcarts equipped with oversized speakers. Throughout the day, the latest dance hits echoed through busy shopping streets, bus terminals, and traditional markets, attracting pedestrians and creating lively public listening spaces.

Vendors competed for customers by playing the latest hits at high volume through oversized speakers, making popular songs instantly recognizable to anyone passing by.

Long before streaming algorithms recommended songs, the streets themselves served as an informal music discovery platform.

---

# A Gateway to Global Music

During the late 1980s and early 1990s, imported albums were expensive and often difficult to obtain.

Gilboard emerged as an informal music market that gave listeners easier access to a wide variety of music, including:

- American pop
- Eurodance
- New Jack Swing
- Hip-hop
- Japanese pop
- Hong Kong pop
- Emerging Korean dance music

For countless young listeners, Gilboard became their first exposure to global music trends that would later influence Korean artists and producers.

---

# The Birth of Korea's Mix Tape Culture

Gilboard offered more than simple cassette duplication.

Many vendors created their own compilation tapes, selecting popular songs from different artists and arranging them into continuous listening experiences. Dance tracks were often edited into medleys, transitions were shortened, and tempos were occasionally adjusted to maintain energy.

These homemade compilations became some of Korea's earliest forms of locally produced mix tapes.

Alongside the famous Highway Medleys (고속도로 메들리) sold at highway rest stops, these tapes became especially popular among drivers and travelers, making dance medleys a familiar soundtrack on Korea's highways during the 1990s.

---

# Gilboard and the Rise of Korean Dance Music

Gilboard played an important role in popularizing dance-oriented music during the early 1990s.

Many artists who later became household names—including Hyun Jin-young, DEUX, Noise, Roo'ra, R.ef, DJ DOC, Turbo, and Two Two—were already circulating widely through Gilboard culture, where energetic dance tracks spread rapidly through cassette compilations and street vendors.

Songs that were played repeatedly through Gilboard speakers often became indicators of street-level popularity, sometimes gaining widespread public recognition before receiving extensive exposure on television.

As a result, Gilboard helped accelerate the spread of contemporary dance music throughout Korea.

# The Other Side of Gilboard

Despite its cultural significance, Gilboard was built largely upon unauthorized duplication of copyrighted recordings.

During this period, public awareness of copyright protection in South Korea was considerably lower than it is today. Many consumers viewed inexpensive cassette copies as an affordable way to enjoy popular music, while the legal and commercial music industry was still developing.

However, widespread piracy reduced legitimate album sales and limited the income received by artists, composers, producers, and record companies. The popularity of Gilboard often made it difficult for record companies to estimate genuine consumer demand, complicating investment decisions and artist development.

From today's perspective, Gilboard serves as both an important cultural phenomenon and a reminder of why intellectual property protection became increasingly important as Korea's cultural industries matured.

---

# The End of the Gilboard Era

In the mid-1990s, South Korea strengthened copyright enforcement through revisions to laws governing recorded media, making unauthorized cassette markets increasingly difficult to sustain.

At the same time, legitimate CD distribution expanded rapidly, giving consumers greater access to officially released albums. In the following decade, digital music services and portable MP3 players fundamentally changed the way people consumed music.

The disappearance of Gilboard was therefore not simply the decline of a street market. It reflected the broader modernization of Korea's music industry, where stronger copyright protection and legal distribution became essential for supporting creators and building a sustainable cultural economy.

# Legacy

Gilboard is remembered today not only as a symbol of Korea's vibrant street music culture, but also as an important reminder of how stronger copyright protection became essential to the long-term growth of the country's music industry.

It introduced generations of listeners to new musical styles, helped accelerate the spread of dance music, and reflected the energy of Korea's rapidly changing youth culture during the late twentieth century.

Understanding Gilboard offers valuable insight into how Korean popular music developed long before K-pop became an international phenomenon.

### Curator's Note

Gilboard remains one of the most fascinating chapters in Korea's musical history because it represents both innovation and contradiction.

It was a place where young people encountered new sounds, global trends, and emerging Korean artists through an energetic street culture unlike anything found today. Yet it also existed within a period when copyright protection was still evolving, limiting the growth of a sustainable music industry.

Remembering Gilboard is not about celebrating piracy. Rather, it is about understanding a unique moment in Korea's cultural history—a period that shaped the listening habits of a generation while also highlighting the importance of protecting the creators whose work made that culture possible.

Understanding Gilboard means understanding both the passion of Korean music fans and the evolution of a modern music industry built on protecting the artists who create it.
