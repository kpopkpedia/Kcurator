When people become familiar with another country's stories and music, their curiosity often extends beyond entertainment into everyday life.

For decades, Hallyu was understood primarily through television screens and concert halls. Viewers tuned in for Korean dramas, and fans gathered for live music performances. Over time, however, that cultural interest began influencing broader consumer choices—from food and skincare to fashion and language study.

This marked an important shift. Hallyu was no longer simply something audiences watched or listened to. For many international audiences, elements of Korean culture were becoming part of everyday experience.

Welcome to **Part 4** of our five-part series exploring the historical evolution of the Korean Wave.

---

## Post Highlights

### Cinema and International Recognition

From early domestic blockbusters to Bong Joon-ho's Oscar-winning *Parasite*, Korean cinema demonstrated that stories rooted in Korean society could achieve broad international critical and commercial recognition.

### Digital Webtoons and Unscripted Media

The rise of global streaming and mobile platforms helped Korean webtoons and unscripted programs reach new audiences, while successful digital stories increasingly moved across television, film, animation, and games.

### K-Beauty and Fashion

Products such as cushion compacts and sheet masks became internationally familiar, while Korean actors and K-pop artists gained increasing visibility in global fashion marketing.

### K-Food and Culinary Culture

Korean cuisine moved beyond specialist communities and became increasingly visible in mainstream dining, grocery, and convenience-food markets around the world.

### The Growth of Korean Language Study

Cultural interest also encouraged more people to study Korean, turning entertainment consumption into a deeper form of cultural engagement.

## Chapter 1: Cinema and International Recognition

**OLDBOY (2003) Official Trailer**

Long before streaming platforms introduced international audiences to Korean television series, Korean cinema had already begun building an international reputation for genre experimentation and distinctive storytelling.

Beginning in the late 1990s, filmmakers such as Kang Je-gyu, Park Chan-wook, Bong Joon-ho, and Kim Jee-woon produced works that expanded conventional narrative boundaries. Films such as *Shiri* (1999), *JSA: Joint Security Area* (2000), *Oldboy* (2003), and *Memories of Murder* (2003) demonstrated that Korean productions could combine commercial appeal with narrative complexity.

International recognition followed. Park Chan-wook's *Oldboy* won the Grand Prix at the 2004 Cannes Film Festival, becoming one of the films that brought greater international attention to the new generation of Korean filmmakers.

The trajectory reached another landmark in 2020, when Bong Joon-ho's ***Parasite*** (기생충) won Best Picture at the Academy Awards. It became the first non-English-language film to receive the award.

What made *Parasite* especially significant was that it did not depend on removing its Korean context for international audiences. Its architecture, family relationships, social hierarchy, and economic tensions were deeply connected to contemporary Korean society. Yet its examination of inequality proved recognizable far beyond Korea.

The film's success reinforced an idea that had been developing for years: Korean stories did not necessarily need to become less Korean in order to travel.

## Chapter 2: New Formats: Webtoons and Unscripted Media

Hallyu's expansion was not limited to established media such as television, film, and recorded music.

As media consumption increasingly shifted toward smartphones and streaming services, South Korean creators and platforms developed new ways of producing and distributing stories.

### Digital Webtoons

South Korea played a central role in developing and popularizing the vertical-scroll digital comic format known as the **webtoon**, designed particularly for reading on computers and, later, smartphones.

Platforms such as Naver Webtoon and KakaoPage helped create digital ecosystems in which successful stories could move across media.

Webtoon-based series such as *Sweet Home* and *All of Us Are Dead* reached global streaming audiences, while franchises such as *Solo Leveling* demonstrated how Korean web novels and webtoons could expand into animation, games, and other media.

This created a new pipeline for Korean intellectual property. A story could begin as a web novel or digital comic, build an audience online, and later become the foundation for a television series, film, animation, or game.

### Unscripted Entertainment

Korean variety programs also developed international audiences.

Long-running programs such as *Running Man* established dedicated overseas fan communities well before the current streaming era. Global platforms later made Korean unscripted programming substantially easier to access.

Netflix's *Physical: 100* provided a notable example. The competition series became the first Korean variety show to reach No. 1 on Netflix's Global Top 10 TV (Non-English) list and entered the Top 10 in 82 countries.

Its success suggested that the international reach of Korean television was no longer dependent only on scripted dramas. Reality, competition, and other unscripted formats could also travel across linguistic and cultural boundaries.

## Chapter 3: K-Beauty and Fashion in Global Marketing

As international audiences became more familiar with Korean dramas and K-pop, many also became curious about the products, clothing, and lifestyles they encountered on screen.

Beauty became one of the most visible examples.

Products such as BB creams, cushion compacts, sheet masks, and multi-step skincare routines became widely associated with K-Beauty. Korean beauty brands gained international attention through an emphasis on hydration, lightweight formulations, product innovation, and relatively accessible pricing.

The influence worked in both directions. Korean cosmetics benefited from the international visibility of Korean entertainment, while beauty products gave consumers a way to engage with Korean culture outside the entertainment itself.

Fashion followed a similar pattern.

Seoul neighborhoods such as Hongdae and Seongsu became increasingly visible as reference points for youth fashion and street style, while Seoul Fashion Week attracted greater international industry coverage.

At the same time, major luxury fashion houses increasingly appointed Korean actors and K-pop idols as global ambassadors.

The development reflected a broader change in global marketing. Korean entertainers were no longer visible internationally only as representatives of Korean entertainment. They were increasingly positioned as faces of global consumer brands.

## Chapter 4: Culinary Integration and Dining Culture

Food represents one of the most direct ways people experience another culture.

As Korean dramas, films, and variety shows reached wider audiences, international viewers repeatedly encountered foods that were ordinary parts of everyday life in Korea: barbecue, kimchi, bibimbap, tteokbokki, fried chicken, ramyeon, and countless varieties of banchan.

For some viewers, watching these foods on screen led naturally to trying them.


| Culinary Category | Global Impact |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Korean BBQ (Gogi-gui)** | Popularized communal, table-side grilling. |
| **Fermented Foods (Kimchi)** | Increased international familiarity with Korean fermentation traditions. |
| **Convenience Foods** | Expanded international demand for ramyeon, sauces, and Korean-style snacks. |


Korean cuisine became associated internationally with bold flavors, fermented ingredients, shared dishes, and communal dining.

At the same time, access changed. Korean restaurants became increasingly common in major cities, while supermarkets and online retailers made ingredients such as gochujang, kimchi, seaweed, frozen dumplings, and instant ramyeon easier to find outside Korea.

This was an important development in Hallyu because food does not require continued participation in fandom. Someone does not need to follow a K-pop group or regularly watch Korean dramas to order Korean fried chicken, keep gochujang in the refrigerator, or cook ramyeon at home.

At that point, a cultural product has begun to separate from the media that originally introduced it.

## Chapter 5: Language Acquisition and Cultural Engagement

Perhaps one of the clearest indications of cultural influence occurs when audiences decide to learn another country's language because they want to understand its culture more directly.

For many international learners, Korean dramas, films, music, webtoons, and online communities provided that motivation.

People began studying Korean to understand lyrics without translations, watch media with less dependence on subtitles, communicate within fan communities, travel, or engage more directly with Korean culture.

The scale of this interest has become increasingly visible on digital learning platforms. In Duolingo's 2025 Language Report, Korean ranked as the sixth most-studied language globally, moving ahead of Italian.

Language learning represents a different level of cultural engagement from simply watching a program or listening to a song. It requires time, repetition, and sustained interest.

In that sense, the growing interest in Korean language study illustrates one of Hallyu's most significant transitions.

The audience is no longer simply receiving Korean cultural products.

Some are actively trying to understand the language in which those products were created.

## Looking Ahead

Television dramas and K-pop may have provided the first encounter with Korea for many international audiences, but Hallyu did not remain confined to entertainment.

Cinema reached international award stages. Webtoons created new sources of global intellectual property. Korean beauty and fashion entered international consumer markets. Korean food became increasingly familiar, and cultural interest encouraged more people to study the Korean language itself.

Together, these developments reveal how the Korean Wave gradually moved beyond the screen.

## Curator's Note

Perhaps the most important change in Hallyu happened outside the screen.

Watching a Korean drama or listening to a K-pop song is still an act of entertainment. But trying a dish seen in that drama, buying a skincare product, learning Hangul, or recognizing a Seoul neighborhood by name represents something different. Culture has moved from something to be watched into something that can become part of everyday experience.

This may be one of the clearest signs that Hallyu has entered a new stage. Its influence is no longer measured only by television ratings, album sales, or concert attendance, but also by the small choices people make after the screen is turned off.
